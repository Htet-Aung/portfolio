## Turn surplus discovery into something people want to explore

Surplus goods can still be useful, but finding the right buyer requires more than displaying a clearance list. ROLLOVER explores a different experience: describe who you’re shopping for, set your limits, answer a playful quiz, and discover a bundle through an arcade-style reveal. The challenge is keeping the surprise relevant, affordable, and easy to inspect.

I co-built the prototype during the 24-hour SDG Open Hack 2026 sprint as part of an equal three-person engineering team. We shared the matching domain, React/Express integration, and interface delivery. My contribution sits within that joint full-stack effort: connecting the product journey to inventory rules and state that remain consistent as users move through the app.

The result is an end-to-end discovery prototype, from setup to a saved session pick. The following screens show how the experience works and the engineering decisions that support it. Inventory and pricing are illustrative; the demo’s brands are examples. The SDG 12 idea is to encourage a second life for usable goods, with no measured waste-reduction outcome claimed.

## 1. Start with a clear invitation

“Good stuff. New game.” introduces the premise before asking the user to configure anything. The capsule-machine illustration, bright colors, strong outlines, and physical-looking controls establish the surplus arcade’s identity. A single **Start my drop** action gives the page a clear next step.

The cover repeats here because this screen sets up the whole interaction. Its left-hand rail previews the seven stages—Who, Budget, Category, Quiz, Boxes, Reveal, and Summary—and later marks progress and lets users return to available stages. That gives an unfamiliar shopping format a visible structure: the personality comes from the visual system, while the route through the experience stays understandable.

## 2. Set the limits before shaping the surprise

The user first chooses **For me** or **Someone else**, then sets a spending ceiling. On **Shape the mix**, they select categories and choose whether to include preloved goods. Size, dietary preferences, and excluded allergens provide more specific boundaries where relevant. Recipient choice also changes how the quiz addresses the person receiving the drop.

These choices become domain inputs. The Express backend reads an Excel inventory workbook with `xlsx`, normalizing product records into an in-memory catalog. Before building bundles, it checks active status, stock, valid affordable prices, and the requested category, condition, size, and food restrictions. Keeping these rules on the server connects the interface’s promises to the products it can actually offer.

This distinction matters: a quiz answer can make an eligible item more appealing, but it cannot make a disallowed size or excluded allergen acceptable. Editing the setup clears the existing offers and reveals so the user’s new limits cannot be paired with an old result. That is the kind of state handling needed when a multi-step form becomes a real product workflow.

## 3. Gather preferences through a quiz people might enjoy

Instead of presenting a long product-preference checklist, ROLLOVER lets users choose a familiar topic and answer scenario questions. In this capture, a **Movies & TV** prompt asks which role they would take during an Interstellar docking scene. The choices express different inclinations; they are not scored as right or wrong answers.

Each answer carries tags from eligible inventory. The topic and previous answers inform subsequent prompts, while selected tags later contribute to product relevance scores. The quiz therefore has a purpose beyond decoration: it translates a conversational interaction into structured inputs the matching engine can use.

The quiz service supports inventory-constrained AI wording, curated questions, and a deterministic fallback. Generated responses are checked for four labeled options with distinct, allowed tags before use. If generation is unavailable or unsuitable, built-in questions keep the journey moving. The model’s role is limited to quiz content; product names, prices, availability, and bundle construction come from the catalog and matching code. This keeps an optional AI interaction from becoming a dependency for completing the experience.

## 4. Compare meaningful offers while keeping the reveal intact

The quiz leads to sealed bundles with names such as **Little lift**, **The remix**, **Wild card**, and **Full rotation**. Before choosing, the user sees price, item count, categories, color hints, and teasers. In this screenshot, **The remix** offers four finds for **$65.10** under a **$1,000** limit. The ceiling is a limit, rather than a requirement to spend it all.

Underneath those cards, the matcher starts with the eligible pool, scores quiz relevance, and constructs bundles around affordable anchor items. Different profiles target different budget fractions and item counts. Category variety and overlap penalties help the offers provide distinct choices; duplicate bundles are skipped, and a sparse catalog can produce fewer than four candidates. Totals are calculated in integer cents to avoid floating-point drift in the budget checks.

The preview API, `/api/drops/match`, returns enough information to compare offers while leaving full product records for the reveal response. This is a useful product boundary: shoppers can assess cost and general fit before choosing, while the discovery mechanic still has something to uncover. It also shows how recommendation logic, money handling, and an API contract work together to support a visible feature.

## 5. Make the opening satisfying and the result consistent

Selecting a box moves into the staged opening: a layered CSS box, lid movement, and confetti give the transition a physical quality. The frontend requests the selected offer’s products while the animation runs. It advances when both the opening is complete and the server response is available, preventing the next screen from arriving without its contents.

`/api/drops/reveal` recomputes the candidates from the request facts and looks up the selected offer ID. That ID is derived from a SHA-256 hash of the bundle and matching inputs. With a stable catalog and the same inputs, the preview and revealed products refer to the same offer. The animation presents that result; it does not select the products.

Users can skip the opening, return to the boxes, or retry a failed reveal. Request IDs prevent a late response from replacing the result after the user has moved away or chosen another box. Reduced-motion styling also supports the transition. Together, these details make the polished interaction usable beyond the ideal sequence of clicks.

## 6. Reveal individual finds without losing progress

After the box opens, its contents appear as sealed product cards. Users can uncover them in their preferred order, watch the **finds revealed** counter update, or choose **Reveal all** to move faster. The screenshot captures the moment before any of the four cards has been opened.

The client stores each opened drop and its card-reveal progress against the offer ID. Going back to compare boxes and returning to a previously opened offer preserves its discoveries. This makes exploration possible without forcing users to repeat the opening or losing track of which products they have already seen.

The interaction also handles focus as cards open, directing keyboard users toward the next card or summary action. Buttons use a stationary hit area with a visually moving face, so their pressed effect does not move the target away from the pointer. These are small implementation decisions with a practical result: a tactile interface that remains controllable.

## 7. Turn the surprise into a transparent haul

The summary brings the selected bundle back into a conventional, inspectable format. The same four finds and **$65.10** total carry through from the sealed preview. Each expandable item exposes its product details, including condition, materials, category, and surplus reason; size, food, and image-attribution information are available where relevant.

Here, the expanded Casio watch entry explains the product and its overstock status. That detail connects the circular-retail premise to the individual item: users can understand what they have found and why it belongs in a surplus drop. It also gives the catalog’s structured fields a visible purpose after matching is complete.

**Compare boxes** supports reconsidering the selection, **Replay opening** repeats the presentation, and **Save this drop** marks the pick for the current session. The prototype ends at that decision. Reservation, payment, account persistence, and fulfillment would require additional systems before it could operate as a retail service.

## What this project demonstrates

ROLLOVER brought product design, frontend interaction, backend rules, and AI integration into one coherent journey under a short delivery window. The strongest part is the connection between them: setup changes invalidate old offers, preferences rank eligible inventory, the preview carries through to the reveal, and returning to a box preserves progress. Those behaviors make the interface more than a collection of animated screens.

The architecture also reflects the prototype’s scope. A spreadsheet catalog made stock easy to edit during the sprint, and a separate matching module kept eligibility and bundle logic inspectable. CSS suited the box geometry without introducing a separate 3D rendering system. Deterministic recomputation kept the backend lean, but a changing production catalog would need versioned offers, expiry, and transactional stock reservations. These choices show full-stack judgment about where to invest effort and what a successful prototype still needs to become a service.
